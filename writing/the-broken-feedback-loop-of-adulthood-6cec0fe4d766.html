The Broken Feedback Loop of Adulthood

Gagan Jain · March 28, 2026 · 2 min read

Originally published on Medium.

“Swimming takes only a month to learn, I’ll do it soon.” I said this to myself ten years ago. The month never arrived. Not because I decided against it. There was no decision. No morning where I weighed it and chose otherwise. It simply stayed where I put it, pending, quiet, never urgent enough to move.

Where did the signal go?

For most of childhood, someone was always tracking your progress. A teacher recorded whether you were improving. A coach noticed your form. A parent registered whether you seemed okay. The environment was instrumented. Feedback arrived continuously, even when it was unwelcome.

The cruelty is structural. The more reliably childhood confirmed your behavior, the more completely adulthood’s silence undoes it. Continuous reinforcement produces learning that depends on continued confirmation: when the reward stops, the absence is felt immediately and the behavior collapses. The schedule that made learning easiest is the one that makes unlearning fastest.

Adulthood removes the visible schedule. Not abruptly, but gradually, and without announcement. It does not punish you for the behaviors childhood rewarded. It simply stops confirming them. The friendship you are quietly neglecting will not signal distress until the distance has already become the default. The habit you meant to build remains pending, exactly where you left it, with no record of when it stopped being imminent.

This is not a collection of separate failures. It is one process, unfolding in stages.

In adulthood, the gap between action and consequence grows long enough that the connection weakens enough to stop reliably updating behavior. When outcomes can no longer be attributed to actions, updates degrade. A signal sustained long enough without response stops feeling like noise. It starts feeling like truth, and behavior reorganizes around it.

The system stops updating, not from defeat but from inference: my actions do not determine what happens to me. And by the time that conclusion becomes visible through crisis: the relationship that finally breaks, the body that finally registers the cost, the gap has already compounded beyond the reach of any single adjustment.

The vague, floating dissatisfaction many adults carry often has this cause. It is not laziness. It is not ingratitude. It is the accurate sensation of navigating without instruments that were once provided automatically.

At some point you stopped trying new things and called it knowing yourself. You have a type of person you get along with, refined over years. You stopped meeting people outside it and called it knowing what you value in friendship. The friendships you might have had remain theoretical. They never failed. They were never pursued.

The policy that got you here becomes the only policy you run: high confidence, low variance, endlessly repeated. Not because it is best, but because nothing contradicts it strongly enough to displace it. The better alternative doesn’t disappear. It is never tried. And every repetition increases the distance from it. The distance never feels like a decision.

The steps you’ve been taking all these years never get the small corrections that would have changed them.

The scaffolding came down. Nobody said anything. Nothing replaced it.
