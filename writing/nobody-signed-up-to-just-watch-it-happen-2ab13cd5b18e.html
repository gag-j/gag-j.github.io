Nobody Signed Up to Just Watch It Happen

Gagan Jain · July 13, 2026 · 5 min read

Originally published on Medium.

I used to write a paragraph, read it back, feel the small friction of a wrong word, and fix it. That friction was the work. Now I type a prompt, watch text assemble itself in seconds, and my job has quietly changed to reading output and deciding if it’s good enough. Same article. Different person doing it. I am no longer the one doing it. I am the one checking.

Nothing about the result is worse. Often it’s faster, cleaner, more consistent than what I would have produced on the fourth draft. But something in me refuses to call this satisfying, and it took a while to understand why.

The task didn’t get easier. It got relocated.

It isn’t just writing. Ask a designer what part of the job they loved. Or a carpenter. Or a photographer. Or a programmer. Very few will say they loved approving the finished product. They loved becoming the reason it existed.

For most of working history, effort and outcome were roped together by time. You wrote the draft, sanded the wood, coded the feature, designed the poster, and the time it took was also the proof that you had done something. Slowness wasn’t a flaw in the system. It was the receipt. The gap between action and consequence was short enough that you could feel your own hand in the result. That feeling has a name, and it isn’t pride exactly. It’s closer to authorship.

Automation doesn’t remove the work. It removes the middle of it. The part where a blank page slowly stops being blank, where a rough shape resolves into a specific one, where you’re wrong three times before you’re right. That middle stretch was never the fun part in the moment, but it was where the sense of having made something came from. Skip it, and what’s left on either side is a request and a verdict. You ask, it answers, you judge. There is no middle to be inside of anymore.

You cannot feel ownership over a stretch of time you were not present for.

This is why the fatigue feels different from old-fashioned busyness. It isn’t the fatigue of too much doing. It’s the fatigue of too much switching, too much checking in on things you didn’t build. Approve this, redirect that, review the draft, redirect again. None of it closes a loop the way finishing something with your own hands used to. You are managing a process instead of having a process. And managing, it turns out, was never the rewarding part. It was always the byproduct.

Notice how little time modern work allows you to stay inside a single thought. Before one task develops enough resistance to become absorbing, another demands your attention. AI didn’t create context switching, but by compressing execution into seconds, it made transitions the dominant unit of work. We no longer spend our day doing tasks. We spend our day transitioning between tasks that are already half-built.

Underneath all of this is something surprisingly basic. We need to be able to point at a piece of the world and say, I caused that. Three hours into solving a stubborn problem, when it finally gives, you can trace the exact moment it happened, and know your own hand was in it. When a tool generates ninety-eight percent of the artifact, that line goes fuzzy. You can no longer trace your way from cause to result. That’s the actual cost. Not the missing hours. The missing chain of cause and effect.

Efficiency was supposed to buy back time. Instead it bought back the part of the task that made the time worth spending.

A content designer who spent a week on a poster wasn’t just producing an asset, they were exercising a kind of taste, testing it against resistance, refining it, watching their own judgment shape the outcome in real time. Now the poster exists in one pass, and that judgment has nowhere left to apply itself except at the very end, as a thumbs up or thumbs down on something someone, or something, else made. Same for the copywriter, the illustrator, the editor. The output improved. The doing evaporated. The trade happened so gradually that most of us noticed the efficiency long before we noticed what had quietly disappeared alongside it.

We used to think automation would free us from work. It may instead be freeing us from the very experiences that made work psychologically rewarding. The problem was never that we’re forbidden from doing the work ourselves. It’s that every incentive increasingly asks why we still would. No one is going to wait a week for a hand-crafted poster when a serviceable one exists in a minute. That much is settled. The stability of “this is simply how the work is done” has already quietly moved, and nobody sent a notice.

Maybe that’s why fulfillment has quietly migrated outside work. It’s probably not a coincidence that so many people are rediscovering the physical world right now. Hiking, sports, long drives with no real destination, trips built around being there rather than optimizing the itinerary. None of it rewards efficiency. A tennis set isn’t better for being quicker. A drive doesn’t improve by shaving off ten minutes. There’s no version of a good hike you can generate in one pass and approve.

The body still has to be present for the outcome to count.

For now. Physical intelligence, the kind that could run the trail, climb the mountain, or swing the racket in your place, hasn’t caught up yet. So these activities have quietly become one of the last stretches of middle that still belongs to whoever is doing it. Not because anyone decided to protect it. Because nothing has been able to take it yet.

Inside work, the same instinct shows up on a smaller scale, and without the luxury of an untouched domain. A hobby kept manual on purpose. One part of the job nobody has automated yet, guarded a little more fiercely than it probably deserves. Not a stand against the market, the market was never going to notice. Just a private, mostly unspoken refusal to let every last stretch of doing be replaced by watching.

Outside those pockets, the feeling returns. Maybe that’s why so many of us finish the day feeling strangely absent from our own accomplishments. The work exists. We just don’t remember being there while it became real.

Perhaps the deepest cost of automation isn’t that machines can now create. It’s that humans are slowly becoming spectators to their own work.
