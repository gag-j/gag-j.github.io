Why Does Stability Feel Like Background Noise?

Gagan Jain · February 17, 2026 · 2 min read

Originally published on Medium.

On paper, my job is the stable one. It pays my rent, compounds over time, and offers a clear trajectory. It is the rational choice. And yet the hours I spend on the tennis court, exhausting and ego-bruising and guaranteeing nothing, are the ones I anticipate. The contrast is difficult to ignore.

Why does the thing that funds my life feel ambient, while the thing with no safety net feels vivid and central?

It is tempting to blame feedback loops. Tennis offers immediate causality. I swing, I see the result, I adjust. Work unfolds differently. Effort stretches across months, filtered through meetings, coordination, and review cycles. The sentence I write today does not visibly connect to the chapter that closes half a year from now. The arc is long and the connection between action and outcome is mediated.

But the deeper difference is not about timing. It is about authorship.

On the court, causality is direct. If I improve, the scoreboard reflects it. If I falter, it is traceable to my own decisions. The loop between action and consequence is tight, and because it is tight, I feel unmistakably like the protagonist.

In stable work, causality is negotiated. My effort moves through systems of alignment, incentives, dependencies, and collaboration. Outcomes are collective and distributed. Wins are rarely attributable to a single swing; losses are rarely catastrophic for a single mistake. This dilution of agency is not an accident. It is the mechanism by which stability is produced.

Stability is a silent contract. In exchange for lower variance and predictable compounding, I surrender some degree of authorship. I trade the volatility of being a decisive actor for the reliability of being a component in a larger machine. The system works precisely because no single person’s swing determines the score.

That trade has psychological consequences. Humans remember chapters, not days. We recall the exam that compressed a year of preparation into three charged hours. We remember the message we waited for because it condensed uncertainty and desire into a single moment. Those experiences concentrate agency and stakes.

Most workdays, by contrast, are part of an ongoing manual written collectively. The plot advances, but rarely because of a single decisive act. Variance is dampened. Outcomes are smoothed. The drama is intentionally reduced.

If work feels like background noise, it may not be because it lacks importance. It may be because it is performing its function well. It reduces volatility. It absorbs shocks. It ensures continuity. The absence of drama is evidence of structural soundness.

Tennis feels like a main plot because every swing matters. Work feels ambient because no single swing is allowed to.

The question, then, is not how to make stability thrilling. It is whether I am comfortable with the exchange it demands.

High agency and high variance come together. So do stability and negotiated causality. If I want the protections of one, I cannot be surprised by the texture of the other.

Stability does not feel like background noise by mistake. It feels that way because smoothing the noise is exactly what it was designed to do.
