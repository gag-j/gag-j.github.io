Your Phone Isn’t the Problem

Gagan Jain · May 5, 2026 · 3 min read

Originally published on Medium.

I sit down to work and reach for my phone before the first thought fully forms. Not because something urgent has happened, but because nothing has. The absence of stimulation feels like a gap that needs to be filled, and before I have decided anything, my hand has already moved.

A few minutes later, I am technically working. Tabs open, cursor moving, but the attention feels shallow, easily displaced. The task is not difficult. It is simply not holding. It is tempting to blame distraction. Phones, notifications, short-form content. The usual suspects. But the issue begins earlier.

Distraction does not start with the notification. It starts with the desire to leave.

On the tennis court, the experience is different. My mind is not quiet, it is active, almost conversational. Where should the ball go? If I place it wide, what is the likely return? If my opponent moves cross-court, how do I recover? The thoughts arrive quickly, but they do not feel like interruptions. They feel like a single thread unfolding in real time, each one pulling the next into place.

The same happens when I am learning something new. One idea leads to another. A gap in understanding triggers a question, which leads to refinement. And when that chain holds, something quietly changes, ideas stop feeling like separate things and start feeling like a structure you are building. Things stick not because you are trying to remember them, but because each new thought has something to attach to. At some point, time drops out of awareness, not because the experience is mystical, but because nothing has broken the chain.

In both cases, the structure is the same. Attention continues!

We tend to think of flow as something rare or peak-like, a special state reserved for moments of high performance or intensity. But experientially, it feels much simpler than that. Flow is what thinking feels like when it is allowed to continue. Not faster thinking. Not deeper thinking. Just uninterrupted thinking. The mind does not go silent in flow. It becomes single-threaded.

Most of the time, that thread does not survive. A task begins, but before the loop can close, before action leads to feedback and adjustment, the attention shifts. A notification appears. A tab opens. A thought about something else intrudes. The chain resets, and we are back at the beginning.

We do not struggle to focus. We struggle to continue.

Leaving has become nearly frictionless. The cost of exit is close to zero, and what you exit toward is almost always more immediately rewarding than what you left. This is not a character flaw. The brain responds to novelty with a small, reliable signal of interest, a nudge toward the new thing. Unresolved work does not offer that. It offers the mild discomfort of incompleteness, of not yet knowing, of a loop that has not closed. So the pull toward the phone is not weakness. It is a mismatch between what the brain rewards now and what the work will offer later.

The notification does not interrupt. It justifies a departure already in motion.

This is the part that is easy to skip over. Attention does not just drift. It defects. It moves toward what we are willing to engage with, and away from what we are not. And the reason we are unwilling is usually not that the task is too hard. It is that the early stretch of any task has a particular texture to it. Nothing has clicked yet. The shape of the problem is still unclear, and that unclarity has a physical quality, a kind of restlessness, like holding a question you cannot yet form properly. The mind does not sit easily there. It wants resolution, and if the work is not offering it yet, it will look for it elsewhere.

So we leave. And then we come back. And then we leave again. Most of our time is not spent thinking. It is spent restarting thinking.

Flow feels rare because it requires staying through that early stretch, long enough for the loop to close, for one thought to lead to the next, for the work to become coherent enough to hold attention on its own.

That shift does happen. Most people never find out, because they left a few minutes before it did.
